# California Finally Let the Trucks In. Now the Fight Moves to Court.

*California has started issuing heavy-duty autonomous truck testing permits, with Aurora and Kodiak among the first approved. The technology was ready years ago. What changes now is the rulebook, and the people suing to stop it.*

For most of the autonomous trucking story, California has been the conspicuous absence. Driverless trucks have run commercial lanes in Texas and Arizona for two years. The trucks themselves were never the reason the biggest freight market in the country stayed closed. The reason was a permit that did not exist. As of this month, it does. The California DMV has begun issuing testing permits for autonomous vehicles over 10,000 pounds, and Aurora and Kodiak are among the first to hold one.

This is a bigger deal than another pilot in another state, and for a reason that has nothing to do with the hardware.

## The rulebook was the product

California's updated AV regulations took effect in April 2026 and did something the previous framework refused to: they created a pathway for heavy trucks, not just passenger cars. The pathway is deliberately slow. It runs in three phases, drivered testing first, then driver-out testing, then driver-out deployment, and the current permits sit at the first rung, with a human safety operator required behind the wheel. There are speed and road conditions attached, including a bar on operating where the posted limit is 25 miles per hour or less unless the truck is on a direct route.

None of that is a technical constraint. Aurora and Kodiak have both logged driver-out miles elsewhere. The phased structure is a political instrument: it lets the state say yes without saying yes to an empty cab tomorrow. For the companies, a drivered California permit is worth more than a driver-out permit in a smaller market, because California is where the freight is. Getting on the first rung is the whole game, because the rest of the ladder is now visible.

## Why this changes the map, not just the roster

We have covered the company milestones before: Aurora going driverless with McLane, Kodiak pushing into the Midwest, Einride putting cabless trucks on Ohio roads. Those were stories about capability. This is a story about access. A permitting pathway in California turns autonomous trucking from a Sunbelt curiosity into something the national shippers have to plan around, because a lane that touches California is a lane most large networks actually run.

The strategic tell is that the first movers are the incumbents of this small field, not new entrants. The barrier California just lowered was regulatory, and the companies positioned to clear it first are the ones who spent the last two years building the compliance and safety-case machinery, not just the driving stack. That is the quiet lesson for anyone watching the space: in a permitted market, the moat is the paperwork as much as the perception system.

## The Teamsters moved the fight, they did not end it

The other thing that happened this month is that Teamsters California sued the DMV. Filed on 5 August, the suit does not argue that the trucks are unsafe in the abstract; it argues that the state got there the wrong way. The union claims the DMV used a rulemaking track meant for changes worth less than 50 million dollars in first-year impact, which let it skip a fuller economic and safety review and narrowed the window for public comment.

Whatever the merits, the procedural angle is the smart one, because it does not need to win to matter. A rulemaking challenge can slow the phased timeline, force a re-do of the analysis, or extract conditions, none of which require proving the technology dangerous. For logistics planners, this is the real variable. The permits tell you California is open. The litigation tells you the pace from drivered testing to an empty cab is now set in a courtroom, not a test track.

## What to actually watch

Stop tracking permit announcements as if each one is a breakthrough. The drivered permit was the expected first step; it is confirmation, not surprise. The signals that matter next are narrower: the first driver-out permit application in California, any court order touching the DMV's rulemaking, and whether the phased conditions get tightened as they scale. Those three will tell you more about when autonomous freight becomes a line item in a California network than any number of miles logged with a safety driver still aboard.

The trucks were ready. California was the gate. The gate is open, and now the argument is about how fast it swings.
