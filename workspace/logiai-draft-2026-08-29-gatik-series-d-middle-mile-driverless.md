# Gatik Raised $200 Million on the Boring Part of Driverless Freight

*While robotaxis burned cash chasing open cities, Gatik took the fixed, repeatable middle mile. The $200 million Series D is a bet that the dull route is the one that pays.*

The headline number is $200 million. The number that matters is $600 million. That is the contracted revenue Gatik says it has already booked, against 85,000 fully driverless orders run for Walmart, Kroger and PepsiCo. Autonomous trucking spent a decade selling the long-haul dream and the open-city robotaxi. Gatik sold neither. It took the middle mile, the short, fixed, high-frequency runs between distribution centers and stores, and turned it into revenue while louder companies were still counting disengagements.

The Series D, led by the Qatar Investment Authority and Koch Disruptive Technologies with Millennium Management, ARK Invest and Intact Private Capital alongside, brings Gatik to roughly $500 million raised. The company runs dozens of driverless trucks today and wants past 100 by the end of 2026. Those are not demo trucks. They move ambient, refrigerated and frozen freight on the same regional lanes every day, which is exactly why the autonomy is tractable.

## Why the constrained route is the whole strategy

A robotaxi has to handle any street, any rider, any hour. A Gatik truck runs a route it has driven thousands of times, between two fixed points, on a schedule. The operating domain is small and known. That constraint is not a limitation Gatik is trying to escape; it is the product. Narrow the world enough and the driving problem becomes solvable, insurable and, crucially, sellable to a shipper who wants freight moved, not a science project. The middle mile was dismissed as unglamorous. It was also the one segment where the economics closed.

## Why this matters in Europe even though Gatik does not run here

Gatik operates in North America, on the retail and grocery lanes of Walmart, Kroger and PepsiCo. European operators do not buy Gatik trucks. They should still read the model closely. Europe runs on exactly this shape of freight: dense hub-to-store and DC-to-DC networks for grocers and 3PLs, short regional legs, predictable timetables. The regulatory and labor context differs, and the EU driver shortage is structural rather than seasonal. The lesson is not the truck. It is the segment choice. The first place autonomous freight pays in Europe will be the same constrained, repeatable middle mile, not the motorway long-haul that gets the press.

## What to watch over the next twelve months

Three signals separate a real inflection from another funding-cycle spike.

- **Trucks running driver-out, not the fleet headline.** The number that counts is how many trucks run with nobody in the cab on live commercial routes, and whether that count clears 100 as promised.
- **Customers beyond the anchor three.** Walmart, Kroger and PepsiCo are ideal early adopters with dense networks. Watch whether a fourth and fifth enterprise sign on, and whether any run outside grocery and retail.
- **Unit economics per lane.** Contracted revenue is a strong signal; margin per driverless mile is the one that decides whether this scales or stalls at the pilot frontier.

Gatik has not proven that driverless freight is a solved business. It has proven that the constrained middle mile is where the money showed up first, and it raised $200 million to press that lead. For a European logistics operator the near-term action is analytical, not procurement. Look at your own network and find the leg that never changes: same two nodes, same schedule, every day. That lane is where autonomy lands in your operation first, whoever ends up selling the truck.
